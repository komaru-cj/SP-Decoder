# Collision-Order Results and Analysis

Starting from the same 10-second fine-synchronized candidate database, we select every available independent target-centric overlap group containing \(2, 4, 8, 12,\) and \(16\) packet frames from the real dual-channel captures (279 scenes in total), and apply every decoder to exactly the same IQ samples. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:collision-order}(a), PDR has a clear overall decline as collision order increases, with only small local reversals for individual methods. SP-Decoder provides the highest packet recovery at four of the five orders; PCube is highest at order 12. At order 16, SP-Decoder achieves a PDR of 0.195, compared with PCube at 0.122 and MaLoRaGW at 0.110.

This difference can be explained by how the decoders treat the truncated chirps introduced by asynchronous collisions. In a target-aligned symbol window, the target contributes one complete chirp, whereas asynchronous interferers generally contribute partial chirps whose dechirped energy spreads across multiple FFT bins. CIC searches for an interference-free spectral component by intersecting spectra computed from multiple partial windows. At the \(3\times\) capture oversampling used in this experiment, short-window FFTs have broader main lobes, stronger sidelobes, and coarser boundary resolution. As the number of packet boundaries increases, the spectral intersection becomes progressively less stable and may suppress the target evidence together with the interference.

PCube exploits the phase difference of arrival associated with each FFT peak. With two receive channels, however, only one antenna pair is available, so the multi-pair averaging of PCube cannot be used. More importantly, a bin containing contributions from several partial chirps no longer has the PDoA of a single transmitter. Its measured phase is instead determined by their complex mixture. Consequently, an interference peak can be pulled into the target's PDoA cluster, while the target peak can be pushed outside it. This ambiguity becomes more frequent as the number of peaks grows, and PCube's hard cluster-and-select decision provides little protection once an incorrect peak falls inside the target cluster.

MaLoRaGW faces a different limitation. Its ZF stage can form at most two spatial streams from the two recorded channels, whereas a high-order collision contains many more active packets. The resulting system is therefore spatially underdetermined: packets not included in the two-column channel matrix remain as structured interference, and nearly parallel channel vectors make the selected matrix ill-conditioned and amplify channel-estimation errors. The subsequent frequency-, time-, and channel-ratio clustering must then operate on already contaminated ZF outputs.

CoRa avoids spatial inversion and instead assigns every FFT bin a Bayesian completeness score derived from peak-magnitude deviation (PMD) and half-period-inversion peak deviation (HPD). This is effective only when the complete target chirp retains both the magnitude learned from its preamble and the low-HPD phase structure of a complete symbol. A multi-packet mixture corrupts those two features at the target bin itself: partial interferer chirps contribute complex energy to that bin, increasing HPD and moving its magnitude away from the target's preamble estimate, while an interference bin can accidentally appear more complete and magnitude-consistent. The real-run diagnostic supports this mechanism. Among 429 candidates for which another CRC-valid decoder provides unambiguous payload truth, failed CoRa packets have median target-bin HPD 0.823 and PMD 0.452, compared with 0.367 and 0.098 for successful packets. The bins selected in those failed packets have lower median HPD (0.366) and PMD (0.292) than the true bins, and their median Bayesian score is 2.04 times the true-bin score. Accordingly, the transmitted bin is ranked first in only 42.4% of the 33 raw symbol windows of a median failed packet, versus 84.8% for a successful packet. This separation is not explained by unstable preamble averaging: the median coefficient of variation across the eight preamble peak magnitudes is nearly identical for failed and successful packets (0.549% versus 0.610%). The failure therefore occurs mainly because payload collisions alter the PMD/HPD features, rather than because the expected target magnitude fluctuates across its preamble.

The published posterior also operates outside its training envelope here. The artifact's fixed model is trained at \(N=256\) (SF8) with at most two synthetic interferers, whereas Fig. 11 uses \(N=1024\) (SF10) and target-centric overlap groups of order 2, 4, 8, 12, and 16. Collision order is not instantaneous concurrency, but it still increases the number and diversity of partial-chirp components encountered over a frame. No retraining or adaptation in the published path changes the PMD/HPD posterior for this larger FFT and multi-interferer distribution. TnB's BEC stage mitigates isolated decision errors but cannot close packets once the feature mismatch produces many correlated symbol errors. Across all 741 anonymous candidates, 286 (38.6%) fail explicit-header BEC, 303 (40.9%) pass the header but fail payload CRC after BEC, 146 (19.7%) close successfully, and 6 (0.8%) are incomplete at the window boundary. On the truth-conditioned subset, a failed packet contains a median of 13 erroneous meaningful pre-BEC nibbles, while a successful packet has median zero. TnB BEC recovers 31 packets that the ordinary CR4/5 decoder cannot, demonstrating that the required BEC path is active, but its bounded column hypotheses cannot repair the dominant multi-symbol attribution errors.

These CoRa results isolate payload decoding rather than native end-to-end reception. CoRa receives the same anonymous fine synchronization as every other method, so TnB packet discovery is excluded from both its measured time and its failure count. At orders 12 and 16, the selected scenes contain 453 candidates for 1032 planned scene packets and 82 for 304, respectively. The plotted denominator deliberately uses the per-scene minimum of planned and anonymously detected packets, while the numerator contains only CRC-valid identities belonging to the selected manifest collision group. This shared-front-end loss does not by itself explain CoRa's reported PRR, but it means that the curve must not be interpreted as native CoRa/TnB end-to-end PRR.

In contrast, SP-Decoder does not attempt to invert the complete multi-packet channel or assign every peak to a transmitter using a fixed scalar feature model. It retains the target's full complex spatial direction---including both inter-channel amplitude and phase---and treats energy orthogonal to that direction as negative evidence. Thus, strong interference-induced FFT peaks can be rejected through their residual spatial inconsistency even when they cannot be individually separated or clustered.

Figure~\ref{fig:collision-order}(b) shows that SP-Decoder has the lowest mean post-synchronization processing time at every tested order, ranging from 30.5 to 102.1~ms per collision scene. On the order-16 input, PCube and MaLoRaGW require 193.2~ms and 477.4~ms, respectively, while SP-Decoder requires 30.5~ms, corresponding to reductions of 84.2\% and 93.6\%. PCube performs peak extraction and PDoA clustering for every payload symbol, while MaLoRaGW additionally updates the active channel matrix, evaluates a pseudoinverse, and clusters peaks using multiple features. SP-Decoder instead configures \(\widehat{\lambda}_u\) once from packet-level reliability and spatial geometry. The payload stage then applies the same signed spatial score to every symbol, without partial-window enumeration, per-symbol parameter search, iterative reconstruction, or repeated multi-packet spatial inversion.

> **Measurement scope.** Collision order denotes the number of packet frames in a target-centric temporal-overlap group, rather than the instantaneous number of simultaneously active signals. All reported decoder results start from the same 10-second fine-synchronized candidate set and use identical dual-channel IQ input. PRR only credits decoded identities that belong to the selected manifest collision group.
